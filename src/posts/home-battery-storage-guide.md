As more Tasmanian households look to reduce reliance on the grid, battery storage has become one of the most common questions we hear from solar customers. If you already have panels installed, adding a battery is often the single biggest upgrade you can make to increase your energy independence, and it's a question that comes up in almost every conversation we have about existing systems.

In simple terms, a battery stores the solar energy your panels generate during the day so you can use it after the sun goes down, during peak evening demand, or in the event of a power outage. Instead of exporting excess energy back to the grid for a comparatively low feed-in tariff, you store it and use it yourself later, which is where most of the financial benefit comes from over the life of the system.

## How a battery actually changes your energy use

Without a battery, a typical solar household exports a large portion of its daytime generation back to the grid, simply because most families aren't home using power in the middle of the day. That exported energy is usually paid back at a fraction of what you'd pay to buy electricity back at night. A battery closes that gap by storing the surplus and releasing it during the evening peak, when the household is actually drawing the most power for cooking, heating, lighting, and appliances.

For many households, this shift alone can meaningfully change the shape of a power bill, particularly for families who are out at work or school during the day and home using energy from late afternoon onward, exactly when solar generation is winding down for the day.

## Sizing a battery to suit your home

The right battery size depends on a few factors: your household's evening energy use, the size of your existing solar system, and whether you're aiming for partial backup during outages or genuine off-grid capability. Commercial customers have additional considerations again, including load-shifting to avoid peak demand charges that can apply to business electricity accounts.

Oversizing a battery is a common and costly mistake. A battery that's larger than your typical daily solar surplus will rarely reach full charge, meaning you've paid for capacity you're not actually using. On the other hand, undersizing means you'll still be drawing from the grid earlier in the evening than necessary. Getting this balance right is really a question of matching the battery to your household's actual usage pattern, not simply buying the biggest unit available.

## Getting the most from a battery once it's installed

Installing a battery isn't the end of the conversation, it's really the start of getting the most out of your solar investment. Simple habits like running high-draw appliances such as dishwashers or washing machines during the day, when solar generation is at its peak, can further reduce how much stored energy you need to draw down each evening. Many battery systems also allow you to set preferences around backup reserve, so you can decide how much charge to keep in reserve for outages versus using for daily savings.

For most homes, a mid-sized battery paired with an existing solar system offers the best balance of upfront cost and day-to-day benefit.
